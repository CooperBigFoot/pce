## 5. Structural themes — my independent read

These are the eight problems the individual findings are symptoms of. Numbering is priority order.

**T1 — The orchestrator is the workflow's unspecified region.**
`SKILL.md` specifies Codex exhaustively (prompt templates, sandbox flags, schemas, one-commit policy) and Claude gates exhaustively (verdict schema, `self_sufficiency` items, delegation contract). It specifies the orchestrator with prose — "active coordinator, not a router, and not an implementer" (`:29`) — and then leaves unspecified everything the orchestrator actually does. Two faces, one cause. *Authorship:* the orchestrator writes `state.json` facts, `vision.md` amendments, revision directives derived from unverified critic notes, and the "already mechanically verified, skip this" lists inside plans — and this is the most privileged text in the system, because a zero-context executor cannot distinguish an orchestrator assertion from ground truth. R5's observation is the sharp one: the skip-list "is the only place a zero-context executor is told to *skip* a real acceptance check." Four of five reports hit this; severity climbs from medium (R3) to critical (R5); the surface widens each time. *Behavior:* the orchestrator improvised an entire operational validation phase with its own `state.json` phase values and a lower-rigor merge path (R1), a filesystem verdict channel used for fourteen dispatches (R3), a red phase (R3), an executor escalation terminal state (R5), a retry policy including whether a failure consumes a cap round (R5), a withdrawal disposition (R5, human-supplied twice), an outage backoff (R1) and a mergeability-race check (R5). Every improvisation was competent. That is the problem: **rigor became a per-instance judgment call rather than a chosen policy** — R1 says it exactly, "review-rigor inconsistency between phases is undocumented rather than chosen" — and R5 names the failure mode this invites, "under repeated failure the tempting shortcut is to self-verify and proceed, which would silently drop the gate."

**T2 — Falsifiability is not modelled anywhere in the workflow.**
The system validates a check by whether it produced output, never by whether it *could have failed*. The class recurs at every altitude and in every repo: a `git grep` whose pattern spans a hard-wrapped line; `cargo … | tail` inside an `&&` chain; a contract `build` command that fails at base and was therefore never a gate; `git diff --check` before `git add`; a merge gate requiring output libtest does not emit under `--nocapture`, which would have rejected a successful five-minute planetary carve; `cargo check` as a freshness gate over 21 latent failures; and mocks that "pinned the wrong contract with full coverage." R2 counts 30+ instances in one run and notes that *none was found by reading*. Two consequences worth holding at vision altitude. First, the workflow's single highest-yield mechanism (critics that execute) is high-yield precisely because it is the only place the falsifiability question gets asked — and it gets asked *incidentally*, as a byproduct of prompt style, which is why R3 explicitly refuses to claim it as a workflow property. Second, this is the one theme where the defect and the cure are both already present in the corpus and simply unwired: R2 recorded blocker trajectories by hand, R4 reproduced the vacuous grep at the ref, R2's critics ran plan gates against deliberate falsifications. The workflow reads none of it.

**T3 — Gate scope is drawn around the artifact of record; defects live in composition and in artifact kinds the ladder has no theory of.**
Two failure directions from one cause. *Composition:* every step green and correct, the milestone broken — an M1 test invalidated by an M2 rule, an M2 proof rendered **unconstructible** rather than merely failing, a milestone whose proof exercises a test-only raster source rather than the shipped wiring, a single-node graph silently dropping an acceptance criterion its own milestone summary claims, and a systematic argumentative lean where all 23 spot-checked rows were individually faithful. *Artifact kind:* the ladder is a compile-and-test ladder, so for a docs-only step it is "nearly vacuous" — R4's M5 was green at 769 passed before and after the edit — and structurally blind to changelogs, glossary-bound claims, CI workflows never captured in the contract, and real environments (R1's eight defects through every green gate). In every single instance, the catch came from a scope *wider than the artifact under review*: the milestone gate, a hand-added end-state question, a hand-added glossary check, or an external oracle. None of those widenings exists in the skill. The structural claim: the workflow's unit of review is the unit of authorship, and defects do not respect authorship boundaries.

**T4 — Artifacts freeze at authoring time; the ground moves; nothing re-grounds at consumption time.**
Phase 1 writes `milestones.json` once and Phase 2 consumes it as spec after two milestones have changed a trait signature and added a whole error variant. One `<planning-ref>` in the templates against a ref that advances per milestone — "reading M2's inputs at the original ref would have been actively wrong." Orientation notes recording a doc as stale, sitting inert in `state.json` while the orchestrator re-reads that doc and reaches the opposite conclusion (both the note *and* its contradicting measurement were already recorded). Category labels copied from one milestone's summary into the next's matrix. Counters maintained by hand and drifting out of sync with the artifacts. The consistent shape: **authoring-time validation exists; consumption-time re-grounding does not**, so freshness depends entirely on the orchestrator remembering to say "your input is stale in one specific way" in prose. R3 and R4 show it works when said; R2 shows what happens when it is not.

**T5 — The caps are a proxy for a signal the runs already produce and the workflow never reads.**
Every run recorded the discriminating data by hand and unprompted — `3->1->1->0 blockers, each novel`; per-round prose histories that R5 calls "the most useful part of `state.json` after compaction." The cap counts rounds. R2's consequence is the sharpest: the cap "creates pressure to stop one round early on exactly the artifacts that need another round", and M4's round-4 `BLOCK` overturning three rounds of "no discriminator is possible" arrived *past* the cap. Three reports observed convergence landing exactly *at* three, so the margin between converged and escalated is zero for hard artifacts. But the cap is being asked to do three jobs — detect stuckness, bound runaway cost, decide what happens next — and only bounds cost honestly, which is why R2 (attacking the detector) and R5 (defending the actuator, and supplying the missing WITHDRAW route) read as opposed while being compatible. Underneath both sits a question nobody has tested and both name: is layered defect discovery intrinsic to adversarial review, or an artifact of how critics are prompted? That single experiment determines whether the cap needs changing at all.

**T6 — Facts learned at runtime are homeless, so the knowledge does not travel.**
The pinned contract shape has slots for format/lint/typecheck/test/build/preflight/version_bump/branch_pr and nothing else normative, so everything a run *learns* escapes through three non-portable channels: ad-hoc `state.json` keys (`lockfile_lesson`, `stopwatch_provenance_rule` — R1 reads these as "themselves evidence the contract shape had no home for these facts"), hand-copied prompt boilerplate (six identical `fix_*.txt` blocks; ~ten "do not read at an earlier ref" clauses; fourteen bespoke write-to-disk blocks), and the user's private project memory ("(b) and (c) live only in this user's project memory; a fresh orchestrator without that memory re-derives them by failure"). R1's corrected freshness rule ended up as "tribal knowledge in a Map Note and a memory file rather than a workflow rule" — note that the *program layer* is currently serving as the de facto rule repository for PCE, which nobody proposes deliberately. The nastiest sub-class, from R2: false-zero hazards "produce confident wrong answers rather than errors", and both instances occurred in the orchestrator's own verification, one nearly "correcting" a true statement into a false one. And the corpus contains its own counter-evidence — the one trap that *is* documented (`< /dev/null`, `SKILL.md:47`) was still hit — which means a home for these facts is necessary but demonstrably not sufficient.

**T7 — The cost model is implicit, so every proposed fix is priced in a currency nobody is tracking.**
Three separate high-confidence recommendations each add a full build/test cycle (consumer test suite per producer merge; every contract command run at base; a falsification run per evidence command), and more add per-item justification scaling with artifact size and end-state checks scaling with merged-milestone count — against plans that already reach 49 KB and milestone PR bodies that already cost ~40–58 k tokens each. Meanwhile three reports independently defend expensive constraints on the grounds that *nothing measurably broke* (plan sizes, the cap, a verification record grown to 333 verdict rows against 135 claim rows). Absence of evidence is doing a lot of load-bearing work in both directions: it protects existing cost and it fails to price new cost. This is the theme that will decide which of the ~30 recommendations can actually land, and no report is positioned to see it because each sees one run.

**T8 — The loop these reports exist to close is open, and the reports have started detecting that themselves.**
Zero recommendations from five reports have reached `skills/pce/SKILL.md` (itemised at the end of §4.1). R5 detected it from inside: `--delete-branch` "recurred unchanged, which suggests these reports are not currently feeding back into `SKILL.md`. Worth confirming that loop exists before writing a third report." The cost is already compounding: R5's executive summary is substantially a re-report of R3 at higher severity, R4 spends structure on marking findings **[confirms report 1]**, and R2 paid for the same cap escalation three times in one run. Every run *succeeded* — all five delivered or paused cleanly — which means the reports are the only mechanism converting per-run improvisation into portable rule, and it is the one mechanism unwired. A secondary confound nobody tests: R5's defenses of ceremony (PR bodies, gitignored `planning/`, caps) all come from a prose/LaTeX repo while R1–R4 come from code repos, so some cross-report disagreement may be **domain-dependence rather than genuine contradiction** — which matters enormously if a single skill is meant to serve both.

- **R3:** the 3-round cap; one atomic step for large trait changes (M1/S1 = 14 files, M3/S1 = 10 files, both challenged by critics and upheld as required for a green tree); Codex-authors/Claude-gates; orchestrator owns all git and network; zsh `<ref>:path` mangling.

- **R2:** the three step-plan-cap escalations — "pure ceremony; each required composing an escalation record and a continue decision for a loop that was working as intended."
- **R3** *(low)* and **R4** *(low, priced)*: Codex-authored **milestone** PR bodies for single-step milestones — 41,122 / 57,792 / 44,774 tokens, "no observed defect caught at this stage."

### 6.3 My read

**The no-change decisions split cleanly into two evidentiary classes, and only one of them is sound.**

*Demonstrated containment* — a specific event proves the constraint earned its place. Orchestrator-owns-git: a critic committed a deliverable it was contracted only to review, and this rule alone kept `c9c6e19` local and reversible; separately, no executor cut a tag despite a repo script printing `git tag pourpoint-v0.2.1` as literal advice on stdout. Executor stop-don't-improvise: three clean `BLOCK`s in one step, each preserving its diagnosis, and a `tectonic` sandbox fault that did not become a fabricated green gate. Cold-dispatch-only: verified byte-identical `files_touched` across revision rounds in three graphs. The human-only tag boundary: the run stopped at a red `Docs` before publishing *even with explicit authorization*. **Do not touch any of these**, and note that several are load-bearing for each other — orchestrator-owns-git is what makes R2's mutation prohibition a recovery story instead of a shipped defect.

*Absence of evidence* — "no failure traced to it." R2's plan sizes, R3's cap, R5's 333 verdict rows all rest on this. It is legitimate reasoning under the reports' own standard ("Preserve useful constraints unless the report shows that the constraint caused a specific failure or measurable waste"), but it is asymmetric: it protects existing cost indefinitely while providing no way to price a new one, which is T7. R3's version is the most honest — it states a falsification condition ("re-examine if a future run escalates on cap exhaustion") — and that condition was met two days later by R5, which nonetheless re-filed the same no-change on different grounds. Nobody noticed.

**One no-change decision I think is wrong on its own terms.** R1 declines the `--delete-branch` fix because "memory already carries" the lesson. Personal project memory is exactly the non-portable channel T6 identifies as the failure mode, and the same report elsewhere complains that two of its three Codex traps "live only in this user's project memory; a fresh orchestrator without that memory re-derives them by failure." R1 applies the portability argument to Codex traps and not to merge mechanics; the distinction is cost (both are one line), so it does not survive. R5's recurrence is the empirical confirmation.

**On the ceremony findings.** Only one of them is backed by a *mechanism* rather than a cost count: R2's cap escalations, where the ceremony is provably unproductive because the escalation's own resolution ("converging, not stuck") was derivable from data already recorded. The milestone-PR-body finding is backed by token counts but not by a defect-avoidance comparison, and R5 shows a PR body being load-bearing for a human decision — so the defensible claim is narrow: *a milestone body restating its only step's body is redundant*, not *PR bodies are ceremony*. Framing it the broad way is what produced C4's unnecessary three-report argument.

**The ceremony nobody counted, and I think it is the largest.** Hand-copied per-dispatch prompt boilerplate. R1: identical lockfile and provenance-ordering lines in all six `fix_*.txt` prompts, described as "permanent per-dispatch prompt overhead." R3: "DO NOT read at an earlier ref" plus a summary of what changed, "repeated in roughly ten prompts"; and a bespoke write-your-verdict-to-disk block in every one of fourteen dispatches. R2: environment hazards "hand-propagated into subsequent prompts". No report files any of this as ceremony, because each individual instance is cheap and each is *load-bearing* — remove the boilerplate and the dispatch fails. That is the tell: this is not ceremony, it is **an unbuilt feature paid for in manual repetition every dispatch**, and it is the same repetition F9/T6 describes from the other side. The reports catch the knowledge-portability half and miss the labor half.

**One item filed as a no-change that I would re-open as an open question, not a decision.** R2 defends `/land-ticket`'s isolation from PCE state as worth its ceremony; R4 shows the cost — ref-verified `key_findings` re-derived from source during landing — and asks for the isolation to be *decided* rather than inherited: "it is defensible as a trust boundary, but it is currently an implicit consequence rather than a stated one." Those two are pointed in opposite directions on the same boundary, and R4's framing is the right one. This is the only place the corpus touches the program layer at all, which is itself notable given that R1's most durable lesson (the corrected `freshness_check` rule) currently lives in a Program #117 Map Note rather than in the skill.
